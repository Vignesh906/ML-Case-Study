Project Summary

Unexpected delays across public transport networks and flight corridors cause significant inconvenience for commuters, missed connections, and increased operational costs. While most existing studies focus on only one mode of transport at a time (like flights only or buses only), commuters in real life use interconnected transport systems. This project designs and evaluates a unified Multimodal Transportation Delay Prediction System that predicts delay severity across four major modes: Bus, Train, Metro, and Airplane. We combined authentic historical flight records with multi-city public transit data and live weather information into a standardized dataset of 104,000 trips. We trained and compared five popular machine learning algorithms: Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Support Vector Machine (SVM), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). Random Forest achieved the best overall balance, outperforming naive baselines by a significant margin. An interactive web dashboard built with Python and Flask allows users to input trip details and instantly view predicted delay severity, estimated delay minutes, and key risk factors.

1. Introduction & Motivation

Reliable transportation is essential for daily commuting, commerce, and tourism. When a flight is delayed, passengers often miss their connecting trains or airport buses, causing a domino effect of travel disruption. Traditional transit management relies on fixed timetables or delayed notifications sent after a disruption has already occurred.

Machine learning provides an opportunity to predict delays before departure. By analyzing trip distance, scheduled departure times, passenger crowding levels, historical route punctuality, and current weather conditions (such as fog, rain, or snow), machine learning models can identify trips at high risk of delay in advance.

2. Problem Statement & Target Definition

Instead of treating delay prediction merely as a simple yes/no question, we formulate the problem into three practical, actionable severity tiers that transit operators and commuters care about:

  • On Time (Category 0): Delays between 0 and 5 minutes (normal operational buffer).
  • Moderately Delayed (Category 1): Delays between 6 and 20 minutes (noticeable delay, possible missed connections).
  • Severely Delayed (Category 2): Delays greater than 20 minutes (major disruption requiring rerouting or rescheduling).
Core Project Goal: Build, evaluate, and deploy an accurate, easy-to-use machine learning pipeline that predicts trip delay severity at the time of scheduled dispatch, while clearly identifying which factors (such as weather or peak-hour crowding) contribute most to the delay.

3. Dataset Sources & Collection

A key challenge in multimodal research is finding comparable data across different transport systems. In this project, we integrated two primary data sources alongside a verified weather service:

A Commercial Aviation Dataset

Official 2015 historical flight records from the US Department of Transportation (DOT) Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS). Covers domestic flights across 14 major airlines and 322 commercial airports.

B Public Transit Dataset

Multi-city transit benchmark covering Bus, Train, and Metro trips across 6 international metropolitan areas (Berlin, London, Paris, Tokyo, Singapore, and New York) with 56 distinct transit corridors.

C Hourly Weather Telemetry

Historical hourly weather records from Open-Meteo, matched by exact coordinates and departure hours. Captures temperature and conditions like Clear, Cloudy, Fog, Rain, Snow, and Storm.

D Standardized Multimodal Corpus

A balanced modeling pool of 104,000 samples (26,000 per mode: Bus, Train, Metro, and Flight) ensuring every modality is fairly represented without one mode dominating the others.

5. Data Cleaning & Leakage Prevention

Eliminating Target Leakage: A common mistake in delay prediction is using data that is recorded after the trip has finished (such as actual arrival time, taxi-in time, or trip completion status). If included, the model easily guesses the delay during training, but completely fails in real life because those values are unknown before dispatch.

In our pipeline, we strictly purged all post-departure variables (`ARRIVAL_DELAY`, `ARRIVAL_TIME`, `TAXI_IN`, `TAXI_OUT`, `AIR_TIME`, `ELAPSED_TIME`, and `service_status`). Every feature in our dataset is strictly observable before the vehicle departs.

Missing Value Handling & Standardization: Continuous values (distance, speed, temperature) were scaled using StandardScaler to have zero mean and unit variance. Categorical fields (transport mode, weather condition, day of week) were one-hot encoded.

6. Feature Engineering

Raw data alone is rarely optimal for machine learning. We engineered 22 intuitive predictors grouped into five practical categories:

1 Trip Geometry & Speed

Scheduled travel time, route length in kilometers, estimated travel speed (km/h), log-transformed distance, and route headway frequency.

2 Passenger Crowding Levels

Vehicle passenger count, vehicle seating capacity, passenger occupancy percentage, and passenger crowding ratio above comfort thresholds.

3 Calendar & Cyclical Time

Departure hour, day of the week, month, peak rush hour indicator (morning 7–9 AM, evening 4–7 PM), weekend flag, and cyclical sine/cosine time transforms.

4 Weather & Environmental Data

Ambient temperature in Celsius, categorical weather state (Clear, Cloudy, Light Rain, Heavy Rain, Fog, Snow, Storm), and an adverse weather binary flag.

Historical Delay Baseline: We computed `historical_delay_mean` and `delay_risk_score` using rolling delay averages of prior trips on the same corridor. This gives the model a sense of whether a route is generally punctual or prone to congestion.

7. Chronological Dataset Splitting

Many machine learning projects use random shuffling (e.g., `train_test_split(shuffle=True)`). However, in transportation, trips occurring on the same day share identical weather and traffic conditions. Random shuffling causes "leakage," leading to falsely optimistic test scores.

To test how well our models predict the future, we split the 104,000 trips **chronologically by calendar months**:

8. Addressing Class Imbalance

Because roughly 75% of trips are on time, an unweighted machine learning algorithm can achieve 75% accuracy simply by guessing "On Time" for every single trip, completely failing to detect actual delays.

To solve this, we used Balanced Class Weighting (`class_weight='balanced'`). This penalizes the model more heavily when it misses a delayed trip, forcing it to pay equal attention to moderate and severe delay categories.

9. Machine Learning Algorithms Evaluated

We benchmarked five diverse machine learning architectures to compare simple linear models against tree-based ensembles and distance-based classifiers:

1 Logistic Regression (Baseline)

A standard linear classification baseline. Fast and interpretable, it fits a linear boundary between feature inputs and class probability scores using an L2 penalty.

2 Decision Tree

Builds a tree of if-then rules based on feature thresholds (e.g., "if peak hour and rain, then delayed"). Constrained to a depth of 12 to prevent overfitting.

3 Random Forest (Ensemble)

An ensemble of 200 individual decision trees trained on random subsets of data. The trees vote together, reducing variance and capturing complex feature interactions.

4 Support Vector Machine (SVM)

Finds the optimal separating hyperplane with maximum margin between classes. Uses Platt probability calibration to produce reliable probability scores.

5 K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)

An instance-based algorithm that classifies a trip by finding the 15 most similar past trips in feature space using distance weighting. Intuitive, but slower to query on large datasets.

10. Evaluation Metrics Explained

To evaluate model performance objectively without being fooled by class imbalance, we tracked four primary metrics:

  • Accuracy: Percentage of total correct predictions across all classes.
  • Macro Precision: The average precision across all three classes, measuring how often predicted delays are correct.
  • Macro Recall: The average ability to catch actual delay events across all three classes.
  • Macro F1-Score (Primary Metric): The harmonic mean of precision and recall averaged equally across On Time, Moderate, and Severe classes. This was our primary selection metric because it penalizes models that ignore minority severe delays.

11. Predicting Exact Delay Minutes (Regression)

In addition to classifying delay tiers, our system also estimates the continuous number of delay minutes. We trained a gradient boosted regression model to predict expected delay minutes, tracking:

  • Mean Absolute Error (MAE): The average error in minutes between predicted and actual delay.
  • Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE): Penalizes large errors more heavily, reflecting severe delay misses.
  • P90 Quantile Delay: A safety margin estimate indicating the delay minutes that 90% of trips will stay under, giving passengers a realistic worst-case buffer.

18. Project Limitations

  • Pre-Departure Information Limits: Scheduled flight attributes explain about 15% of flight delay variance. Real-time air traffic control holds and en-route storm diversions happen dynamically while in flight and cannot be known at scheduled departure.
  • Simulation vs. Real Municipal Traffic: While the aviation data is 100% authentic US DOT records, the municipal transit data uses a realistic simulation benchmark based on city timetables.
  • Underground Weather Isolation: In our dataset, underground metro is insulated from surface storms. In the real world, outdoor metro tracks can experience weather delays during severe blizzards or floods.

19. Future Work & Enhancements

  • Real-Time GPS Streaming: Connecting the system to live General Transit Feed Specification Realtime (GTFS-RT) feeds for live vehicle tracking.
  • Mobile Passenger App: Creating an Android/iOS mobile application with push notifications for personalized commuter route alerts.
  • Graph Neural Networks (GNNs): Modeling connected transit stations as a spatial graph to predict how a delay at one station propagates across the entire city.

20. Conclusion

This project successfully built and demonstrated an end-to-end Multimodal Transportation Delay Prediction System covering Bus, Train, Metro, and Airplane services. By combining 104,000 trips across operational, temporal, and weather features, we proved that machine learning provides actionable early warning (+0.2747 Macro-F1 lift over baseline). Random Forest proved to be the most effective algorithm, balancing accuracy and delay recall. The accompanying web application provides an intuitive platform for commuters and transport planners to explore delay risks interactively.
